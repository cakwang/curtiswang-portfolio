What Works? Promising Practices in International Development

Norway's first international Massive Open Online Course (MOOC) brought together a global brain trust, including Francis Fukuyama, to provide an advanced introductory primer on international development. Serving over 12,000 students across 150 countries, the project leveraged learning analytics research to shift the MOOC paradigm away from passive consumption and toward an active, facilitated community of inquiry.

Problem & Learner Context

Unlike traditional instructional design projects designed to fix an existing workplace performance gap, this project was born out of a proactive, prestige-driven academic opportunity. During the peak of the Massive Open Online Course (MOOC) era, Stanford University partnered with the University of Oslo to create a high-profile, multi-institution digital brain trust. The goal was to plant an intellectual flag in the burgeoning MOOC space and provide a comprehensive, high-depth primer on international development aid efforts.

The curriculum was structured around five core pillars: General International Development; Growth and Poverty Reduction; Governance and Rule of Law; Global Health; and Aid & International Cooperation.

The initiative targeted a vast, highly heterogeneous global audience, ultimately attracting over 12,000 students across 150 countries. Because the course was open access and geared toward a general audience, the design team had to account for the unique systemic constraints of open online learning. Rather than adopting a monolithic view of learner completion, the project was intentionally contextualized around established learning analytics research from the Stanford School of Education. This research demonstrated that MOOC populations naturally split into distinct behavioral subpopulations—such as "Completing" and "Auditing" trajectories. By designing with the explicit expectation of low certificate completion rates, the team refocused its strategy away from rigid compliance and toward deep, voluntary social engagement.

My Design Decisions & Rationale

This project operated under a highly agile, two-person instructional design studio framework. Working with a lean footprint, the division of labor was strictly optimized: one partner served as the primary content expert and liaison to the global subject matter experts (SMEs), while I single-handedly managed the end-to-end multimedia production, technical implementation, and asset creation.

The course featured an elite roster of world-class scholars, including Francis Fukuyama and prominent faculty from institutions like China Agricultural University and the University of Malawi. To preserve the intellectual freedom and academic nuance of these high-profile SMEs, they were given autonomy over their primary content structures. My role shifted to that of an editorial guardian of cognitive load. To balance the varying levels of density across modules, I worked closely with instructors to separate introductory concepts from material that depended heavily on prior knowledge. For highly technical domains, I systematically interviewed the SMEs from a beginner’s perspective, prompting them with targeted questions to build the necessary instructional scaffolding within brief video segments.

In many early MOOCs, discussion boards were deployed as a passive, unmoderated afterthought. Drawing on contemporary learning sciences research, we recognized that active social interaction and alignment with personal enrichment goals are primary drivers of learner persistence. We rejected generic forum prompts in favor of highly thematic, open-ended intellectual questions designed to let global learners contextualize the material against their own regional experiences. Crucially, we budgeted and embedded dedicated teaching assistants (TAs) to facilitate, guide, and ground these conversations every evening, transforming a static bulletin board into an active feedback loop.

Project Walkthrough & Highlights

To minimize friction for a massive, self-paced audience, the course architecture utilized a clean, intuitive vertical progression reminiscent of a modern Articulate Rise micro-course. Learners navigated each module through a fixed, three-part structural cadence managed via a persistent left-hand navigation bar.

Step 1: Domain Expert Video Lectures: Highly polished, media-rich video lectures delivered core concepts and case studies from the global faculty roster.

Step 2: Embedded Formative Assessments: Immediately following the video delivery, learners encountered formative checkpoints. These consisted of multiple-choice or reflective, open-ended questions designed to make the audience analyze their own perceptions of international development aid before moving forward.

Step 3: Thematic Forum Links: The final step in the menu directed learners via a direct link to a dedicated discussion board layout. This space functioned similarly to a Reddit thread ecosystem, segregating conversations by specific, curated prompts where TAs actively moderated the entries. A module was marked complete only after the learner traversed all three items in the sequence.

Results & Evidence of Value

While we did not possess a modern, integrated learning analytics suite at the time of launch, behavioral metrics and qualitative reports from the teaching assistants proved the efficacy of our social engagement strategy.

  • Exceptional Baseline Engagement: In the opening week of the course, 15% of the 12,000+ enrolled learners were actively writing and responding to threads within the discussion forums. For a massive open online environment, a 15% active production rate represented a major departure from typical passive lurking behaviors.
  • Sustained Community Vibrancy: While overall engagement numbers experienced the standard decay curve over the lifecycle of the course, the forums never felt empty. TAs reported a consistent, high volume of nightly conversational facilitation.
  • The "Superstar" Phenomenon: The course data empirically validated the Stanford subpopulation framework's profile of highly engaged learners[cite: 11, 378]. The platform revealed a distinct cohort of "superstar" students who mirrored the *Completing* and active *Auditing* trajectories[cite: 131, 134]. These individuals posted in every single thread and persisted with high satisfaction from week one through the end of the course [cite: 135, 287], acting as vital peer facilitators who maintained the intellectual momentum and positive community feedback loops of the discussion ecosystem[cite: 381].

Reflection & Lessons Learned

As an experimental pilot for a lean design studio, the project was an undeniable success in production execution and global scale. However, critical reflection reveals a clear pedagogical gap: we over-indexed on a traditional, one-way lecture paradigm.

Despite the high production value and sophisticated graphics, the video segments essentially functioned as a passive content dump. We defaulted to the standard university lecture format rather than utilizing creative video-production tactics—such as interactive scenarios, direct learner elicitation, or situational framing—that would have broken the fourth wall and driven deeper initial relevance.
